### Q1 · Acute decompensated heart failure

**Keyed answer: C** — Double the IV furosemide dose

This patient has persistent congestion with an objectively inadequate natriuretic response: urine output <150 mL/h and spot urine sodium 38 mEq/L (threshold <50-70 mEq/L) at 2 hours after the loop dose, per the 2019 HFA/ESC diuretic position statement. The cause is underdosing, not diuretic failure — 40 mg IV furosemide is only about the equivalent of his 80 mg total daily oral dose, whereas DOSE-AHF (NEJM 2011) used 2.5 times the home oral daily dose IV and showed greater symptom relief and fluid loss with only transient creatinine rise. The 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF guideline states (Class 2a) that when diuresis is inadequate to relieve congestion, the diuretic regimen should be intensified using higher doses of IV loop diuretic or addition of a second diuretic; escalating the loop dose is the first step because loop diuretics show a threshold dose-response relationship that is right-shifted in decompensated HF. He is warm and well perfused with adequate BP, so there is no indication for inotropes or vasodilators, and the modest creatinine rise during active decongestion is not a reason to de-escalate diuresis.

*Key point:* When congestion persists after an initial IV loop diuretic dose with urine sodium below 50-70 mEq/L, double the loop diuretic dose before adding a second diuretic class. [1,2,3]

<details><summary>Why the others are wrong</summary>

- **A** — Sequential nephron blockade with a thiazide-type diuretic is appropriate only after the loop diuretic dose has been adequately escalated and response remains poor; adding metolazone now risks hypokalemia and hyponatremia while leaving the underdosed loop diuretic uncorrected.
- **B** — IV nitroglycerin is an adjunct for acute HF with hypertension or flash pulmonary edema. With BP 116/70 mm Hg and a subacute congestive picture, vasodilation addresses neither the sodium retention nor the inadequate natriuresis, and may limit further diuretic titration.
- **D** — CARRESS-HF showed ultrafiltration was not superior to stepped pharmacologic therapy and caused more creatinine rise and complications. Ultrafiltration is reserved for true diuretic refractoriness after maximal escalation, which has not been attempted here.
- **E** — ROSE-AHF found low-dose dopamine did not improve decongestion or renal function in acute HF with renal dysfunction. This patient is warm and normotensive, with no evidence of low cardiac output requiring inotropic or 'renal-dose' support.

</details>

*Guidelines cited:* 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA Guideline for the Management of Heart Failure; 2019 HFA/ESC Position Statement on the Use of Diuretics in Heart Failure; DOSE-AHF (NEJM 2011)

### Q2 · Acute exacerbation of asthma

**Keyed answer: D** — Magnesium sulfate, 2 g intravenously

Per the GINA 2025 report, patients with a severe exacerbation who do not respond to the initial hour of intensive inhaled short-acting beta-2 agonist plus ipratropium and systemic corticosteroid may be given a single 2-g IV infusion of magnesium sulfate over 20 minutes; GINA explicitly notes magnesium is not for routine use in all exacerbations but is an option in severe, treatment-refractory obstruction (a "consider" recommendation reflecting moderate-to-low certainty evidence). NAEPP EPR-3 similarly endorses magnesium for severe exacerbations with FEV1/PEF persistently below 40% predicted. This patient satisfies those criteria: she remains dyspneic with accessory muscle use and PEF of only 33% predicted after complete, correctly delivered first-line therapy. Equally important is what her data exclude. She is alert and fully oriented, oxygenating adequately on low-flow oxygen, and her PaCO2 is 33 mm Hg — persistent hypocapnia indicates she is still ventilating effectively and is not in impending respiratory failure. GINA reserves intubation for deteriorating mental status, exhaustion, silent chest, or a normalizing/rising PaCO2, none of which is present; premature intubation in acute asthma invites dynamic hyperinflation, hypotension, and barotrauma. Magnesium is therefore the appropriate escalation while continuing bronchodilators and corticosteroids and arranging monitored (step-down or ICU) observation. Watch for flushing, hypotension, and hyporeflexia during infusion; renal impairment mandates caution. [1,4]

*Key point:* In a severe asthma exacerbation that fails to respond after one hour of inhaled bronchodilators and systemic corticosteroids, add a single 2-g IV magnesium sulfate infusion; intubate only for altered mentation, exhaustion, or rising PaCO2. [1]

<details><summary>Why the others are wrong</summary>

- **A** — GINA does not recommend aminophylline or theophylline in acute asthma: efficacy is inferior to inhaled beta-2 agonists and the therapeutic-to-toxic ratio is narrow, with arrhythmias, vomiting, and seizures. It is an outdated escalation choice. [1]
- **B** — Intubation is not indicated: she is alert, oriented, oxygenating on 2 L, and hypocapnic (PaCO2 33 mm Hg). Airway control is reserved for depressed mentation, exhaustion, silent chest, or a normalizing/rising PaCO2 — the trap of over-reading a modest PEF.
- **C** — Parenteral epinephrine in acute bronchospasm is reserved for anaphylaxis or angioedema-associated asthma. She has no urticaria, angioedema, stridor, or hypotension, and no allergen exposure; it adds tachycardia without benefit over inhaled beta-2 agonists. [1]
- **E** — Routine noninvasive ventilation is not recommended in acute asthma; evidence is low quality, and it can worsen air trapping or agitation. It should never precede or delay pharmacologic escalation such as magnesium in a patient still able to protect her airway. [1]

</details>

*Guidelines cited:* GINA 2025 Global Strategy for Asthma Management and Prevention; NAEPP Expert Panel Report 3 (NHLBI, 2007); Cochrane review: IV magnesium sulfate for acute asthma in adults

### Q3 · Upper GI bleeding

**Keyed answer: C** — Intravenous pantoprazole infusion for 72 hours

Per the ACG 2021 guideline on upper gastrointestinal and ulcer bleeding, patients with an ulcer showing high-risk stigmata (active bleeding, a nonbleeding visible vessel, or an adherent clot) who undergo successful endoscopic hemostasis should receive high-dose PPI therapy — either continuous infusion (80 mg bolus followed by 8 mg/h) or high-dose intermittent dosing — for 3 days (conditional recommendation, moderate certainty of evidence). Sustained intragastric pH above 6 prevents pepsin-mediated dissolution of the platelet-fibrin plug over the 72-hour window when rebleeding risk is highest; this reduces rebleeding, need for repeat intervention, and mortality in high-risk ulcers. This patient has a Forrest IIa lesion (nonbleeding visible vessel), the archetypal high-risk stigma, and has already had combination endoscopic therapy, so the remaining evidence-based step is pharmacologic consolidation. After 72 hours he should transition to oral PPI, have the NSAID permanently discontinued, H. pylori results acted upon, and aspirin resumed promptly for secondary cardiovascular prevention. The same guideline explicitly recommends against routine scheduled second-look endoscopy and reserves angiographic or surgical rescue for failure of repeat endoscopic hemostasis. Transfusion is appropriately restrictive here (threshold 7 g/dL, target 7-9 g/dL) based on Villanueva et al. (NEJM 2013).

*Key point:* After successful endoscopic hemostasis of an ulcer with high-risk stigmata, give high-dose PPI (continuous infusion or intermittent) for 3 days; routine second-look endoscopy is not indicated. [1]

<details><summary>Why the others are wrong</summary>

- **A** — Embolization is rescue therapy for bleeding that persists or recurs after a second endoscopic attempt. Hemostasis was achieved and the patient is stable, so angiography adds procedural and ischemic risk without benefit.
- **B** — Permanent aspirin withdrawal after ulcer bleeding increases cardiovascular events and mortality. ACG 2021 advises resuming cardioprotective aspirin promptly (within 1-3 days) after hemostasis, together with PPI therapy.
- **D** — HALT-IT (Lancet 2020) found no mortality benefit from tranexamic acid in acute GI bleeding and more venous thromboembolic events and seizures; ACG 2021 recommends against its use.
- **E** — Routine scheduled second-look endoscopy is recommended against; repeat endoscopy should be driven by clinical evidence of rebleeding such as recurrent hematemesis, hypotension, or a falling hemoglobin.

</details>

*Guidelines cited:* ACG Clinical Guideline: Upper Gastrointestinal and Ulcer Bleeding (2021); International Consensus Recommendations on Management of Nonvariceal Upper GI Bleeding (Ann Intern Med 2019)

### Q4 · Thyroid storm

**Keyed answer: D** — Potassium iodide solution orally

Per the 2016 ATA hyperthyroidism guideline, thyroid storm requires simultaneous multimodality therapy: a beta blocker, a thionamide (PTU preferred because it also inhibits peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion), inorganic iodide, a glucocorticoid, aggressive cooling and volume support, and treatment of the precipitant (here, an intercurrent respiratory infection). This patient has a Burch-Wartofsky score of 60 and has already received the thionamide, beta blocker, and glucocorticoid; the missing element is inorganic iodide (SSKI or Lugol solution). Iodide is the fastest-acting agent for lowering circulating hormone because it acutely blocks release of preformed T4 and T3 from thyroidal colloid (acute Wolff-Chaikoff effect); thionamides do not affect stored hormone. Both the ATA 2016 and the JTA/JES 2016 statements emphasize that iodide be administered approximately 1 hour AFTER the thionamide, so that organification is already blocked — otherwise the iodine load provides substrate for new hormone synthesis and can transiently worsen thyrotoxicosis (Jod-Basedow phenomenon). One hour has elapsed, so iodide is now appropriate. Persistent fever and tachycardia at 1 hour are expected and do not indicate treatment failure; escalation to salvage measures is reserved for storm refractory to a complete conventional regimen.

*Key point:* In thyroid storm, give a thionamide first, then inorganic iodide at least 1 hour later, together with a beta blocker and glucocorticoid, and treat the precipitant. [1,2]

<details><summary>Why the others are wrong</summary>

- **A** — Amiodarone is roughly 37% iodine by weight and unpredictably alters thyroid hormone kinetics (and can itself cause thyrotoxicosis); it is not the recommended agent for rate control in storm, where a titratable beta blocker such as esmolol — already running — is preferred.
- **B** — Bile acid sequestrants interrupt enterohepatic recirculation of thyroid hormone and are an ATA-recognized ADJUNCT, but only after the core regimen including iodide is in place, or when thionamides cannot be used. It is not the next step.
- **C** — Lithium blocks thyroid hormone release and is an alternative when iodide or thionamides are contraindicated (e.g., iodine allergy, agranulocytosis). Its narrow therapeutic index and renal/CNS toxicity make it inappropriate when standard iodide has not yet been given.
- **E** — Plasma exchange removes protein-bound hormone and is salvage therapy for storm refractory to a complete conventional regimen or when drugs are contraindicated. Persistent fever and tachycardia 1 hour after initiating therapy do not define refractory disease.

</details>

*Guidelines cited:* 2016 ATA Guidelines for Diagnosis and Management of Hyperthyroidism and Other Causes of Thyrotoxicosis; 2016 Japan Thyroid Association/Japan Endocrine Society Guidelines for Thyroid Storm

### Q5 · Infective endocarditis

**Keyed answer: C** — Early surgical mitral valve replacement

The 2020 ACC/AHA valvular heart disease guideline gives a Class 1 recommendation that early surgery (during the initial hospitalization, before completion of a full therapeutic course of antibiotics) is indicated in patients with infective endocarditis who present with valve dysfunction resulting in symptoms or signs of heart failure. The 2023 ESC endocarditis guideline concordantly classifies aortic or mitral native-valve IE with severe regurgitation causing refractory pulmonary edema or cardiogenic shock as an indication for emergency/urgent surgery within hours to days (Class I). This patient has TEE-documented anterior mitral leaflet perforation with severe regurgitation and new acute pulmonary edema despite a normal LVEF — the classic hemodynamics of acute severe MR, in which a small, non-compliant left atrium transmits regurgitant pressure directly to the pulmonary circuit and the murmur is soft and early-peaking. Sterile blood cultures and clinical improvement of infection do not correct a mechanically destroyed valve; mortality in IE with heart failure managed medically approaches 50%, and operating on an actively infected valve carries acceptable prosthetic infection risk. The EASE trial further demonstrated that early surgery in left-sided IE with large vegetations is safe and reduces embolic events. Antibiotics are continued perioperatively, with the postoperative course counted per guideline (full course after surgery when valve cultures are positive).

*Key point:* In infective endocarditis, valve dysfunction causing heart failure mandates early surgery during the index hospitalization, regardless of antibiotic duration or blood culture clearance. [1,2]

<details><summary>Why the others are wrong</summary>

- **A** — Adjunctive gentamicin is not recommended for native-valve staphylococcal endocarditis (2015 AHA statement) — it adds nephrotoxicity without improving cure — and antibiotic escalation does nothing for a perforated leaflet.
- **B** — This is the key trap: waiting to "sterilize" the valve for 6 weeks in a patient with decompensated heart failure from severe regurgitation markedly increases mortality. Heart failure is the most urgent surgical indication, superseding antibiotic duration.
- **D** — Diuresis is reasonable temporizing support but is not definitive; medical therapy alone for IE with valve dysfunction and heart failure carries very high in-hospital mortality and violates the Class 1 early-surgery recommendation.
- **E** — TEE has already established severe regurgitation with leaflet perforation. Repeating imaging in a week delays a Class 1 indicated operation; serial echo is for stable patients without heart failure or local complications.

</details>

*Guidelines cited:* 2020 ACC/AHA Valvular Heart Disease Guideline; 2023 ESC Guidelines for the Management of Infective Endocarditis; 2015 AHA Infective Endocarditis Scientific Statement

### Q6 · Immune thrombocytopenia

**Keyed answer: D** — Romiplostim

This patient has chronic (>3 months), corticosteroid-dependent ITP with steroid toxicity (weight gain, cushingoid features, hyperglycemia). The ASH 2019 ITP guideline recommends corticosteroid courses of no more than 6 weeks including taper, and for adults with ITP lasting ≥3 months who are corticosteroid-dependent or unresponsive it suggests a thrombopoietin receptor agonist rather than rituximab, and a TPO-RA rather than splenectomy (both conditional recommendations, very low certainty evidence). Romiplostim (or eltrombopag/avatrombopag) increases megakaryocyte platelet production, produces high durable response rates, and is steroid-sparing. A second consideration seals the choice here: she is HBsAg negative but anti-HBc positive (resolved HBV). Per the AGA 2015 guideline, rituximab is a high-risk agent for HBV reactivation in anti-HBc-positive patients and requires antiviral prophylaxis; selecting a TPO-RA avoids that risk and the need for prophylactic therapy and monitoring. ASH also suggests delaying splenectomy for at least 12-24 months after diagnosis because late spontaneous remission and TPO-RA response may obviate surgery. Her platelet count of 21,000/µL with only cutaneous bleeding does not require urgent count elevation, so IVIG is unnecessary. Thus, transitioning to romiplostim while tapering prednisone off is the most appropriate next step.

*Key point:* In adults with ITP lasting ≥3 months who are corticosteroid-dependent, ASH suggests a thrombopoietin receptor agonist over rituximab or splenectomy, and prolonged corticosteroids should be avoided. [1]

<details><summary>Why the others are wrong</summary>

- **A** — Azathioprine has slow onset (weeks to months), modest and poorly documented response rates, and is not among the preferred second-line agents in ASH 2019; it would not reliably permit prompt steroid withdrawal.
- **B** — IVIG produces only a transient (days to weeks) platelet rise and is intended for clinically significant bleeding or procedural coverage — not maintenance therapy in chronic steroid-dependent ITP with only cutaneous findings.
- **C** — Rituximab is an accepted second-line option, but ASH 2019 conditionally favors a TPO-RA over rituximab, and her anti-HBc positivity creates HBV reactivation risk requiring antiviral prophylaxis — making it the inferior choice.
- **E** — ASH 2019 suggests a TPO-RA over splenectomy and recommends delaying splenectomy at least 12-24 months from diagnosis; at 8 months, surgery with its infectious and thrombotic risks is premature.

</details>

*Guidelines cited:* ASH 2019 Guidelines for Immune Thrombocytopenia (Blood Advances); International Consensus Report on ITP (2019); AGA Guideline on Prevention/Treatment of HBV Reactivation During Immunosuppressive Therapy (2015)

### Q7 · Hyponatremia

**Keyed answer: C** — Give desmopressin plus 5% dextrose in water

Low urine sodium (8 mEq/L) with urine osmolality 96 mOsm/kg identifies hypovolemic, low-solute-intake hyponatremia (beer potomania). Saline repletion removed the hypovolemic ADH stimulus, unmasking a massive free-water diuresis — 3.8 L of dilute urine — that raised sodium 14 mEq/L in 7 hours. The 2014 ESE/ESICM/ERA-EDTA hyponatraemia guideline limits correction to 10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and 8 mmol/L per 24 hours thereafter, and states that when this is exceeded, clinicians should stop active correction, consult an expert, and administer electrolyte-free water (10 mL/kg 5% glucose over 1 hour) together with desmopressin 2 µg IV, with hourly sodium monitoring [1]. The US expert panel likewise endorses re-lowering when correction overshoots, particularly with ODS risk factors [2]. This patient has serum sodium below 105 mEq/L, hypokalemia, alcohol use disorder, and malnutrition — the classic high-risk cluster — so the stricter 8 mEq/L/24 h ceiling applies and he is already 6 mEq/L beyond it. Desmopressin restores urinary concentration and halts ongoing free-water losses, while dextrose in water replaces the water already lost, bringing the 24-hour delta back into a safe range. Improvement in mental status does not license further rapid correction; ODS is typically delayed 2-6 days and clinically silent at this stage [4]. Note that his hyponatremic encephalopathy has already resolved, so no further hypertonic therapy is indicated; hypertonic saline would be reserved for ongoing seizures or severe obtundation, ideally as monitored boluses [3].

*Key point:* When hyponatremia self-corrects faster than 8-10 mEq/L per 24 hours because of a water diuresis, stop saline and re-lower sodium with electrolyte-free water plus desmopressin. [1,2]

<details><summary>Why the others are wrong</summary>

- **A** — Hypertonic saline is indicated only for active hyponatremic encephalopathy. His symptoms have resolved and sodium is already rising far too fast; 3% saline would compound the overcorrection and increase ODS risk.
- **B** — With urine osmolality near 100 mOsm/kg, isotonic saline is effectively hypotonic relative to the urine being excreted; continuing it feeds the water diuresis and accelerates the sodium rise rather than restraining it.
- **D** — Fluid restriction addresses SIADH-type dilutional hyponatremia. Here the problem is excessive renal free-water loss with an already excessive correction rate; restricting intake would worsen the overshoot.
- **E** — Vaptans promote aquaresis and further raise sodium — the opposite of what is needed. Tolvaptan is contraindicated in hypovolemic hyponatremia and in patients whose sodium is correcting too rapidly.

</details>

*Guidelines cited:* European Society of Endocrinology/ESICM/ERA-EDTA Clinical Practice Guideline on Hyponatraemia (2014); US Expert Panel Recommendations on Hyponatremia (Verbalis et al, Am J Med 2013)

### Q8 · Giant cell arteritis

**Keyed answer: E** — Start intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily

This is cranial GCA with arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy: age >50, new temporal headache, jaw claudication, abnormal temporal artery, PMR features, ESR 92 mm/h, and acute painless monocular vision loss with a pallid, edematous disc and afferent pupillary defect. The 2021 ACR/Vasculitis Foundation guideline makes two directly applicable statements: therapy must be started immediately when GCA is strongly suspected rather than awaiting biopsy (strong recommendation), and in patients with GCA and threatened or established vision loss, IV pulse glucocorticoids are recommended over high-dose oral glucocorticoids (conditional recommendation, low certainty of evidence). The 2018 EULAR large vessel vasculitis recommendations similarly advise 500-1000 mg of IV methylprednisolone daily for up to 3 days for acute visual loss, followed by oral prednisone approximately 1 mg/kg/day with a slow taper. The rationale is fellow-eye protection: contralateral involvement can occur within days, and vision already lost seldom recovers. Because histologic changes persist for roughly 2 weeks after glucocorticoids are begun, temporal artery biopsy (or temporal/axillary artery US showing a halo sign) should be arranged promptly but after treatment. Adjunctive low-dose aspirin, bone protection, and gastroprotection are then addressed, and a glucocorticoid-sparing agent is layered onto the taper. [1,2]

*Key point:* In GCA with threatened or established vision loss, give IV pulse methylprednisolone immediately — never delay glucocorticoids for temporal artery biopsy, which stays informative for about 2 weeks. [1,2]

<details><summary>Why the others are wrong</summary>

- **A** — Tocilizumab is guideline-supported for glucocorticoid sparing and relapse prevention (GiACTA), but it was never studied as rescue for acute ischemic vision loss and its onset is far too slow; it also pairs here with oral rather than IV glucocorticoid. [1,3]
- **B** — High-dose oral prednisone is correct initial therapy for GCA WITHOUT ischemic complications. With established vision loss, guidelines conditionally favor IV pulse glucocorticoids over oral to protect the fellow eye. [1]
- **C** — Delaying glucocorticoids for biopsy risks contralateral blindness within days. Biopsy remains informative for about 2 weeks after treatment begins, so it should follow — never precede — therapy in vision-threatening disease. [1,2]
- **D** — Large vessel imaging screens for aortitis and aneurysm in GCA but neither confirms cranial disease acutely nor treats it; obtaining it first wastes the narrow window for preserving the unaffected eye.

</details>

*Guidelines cited:* 2021 ACR/Vasculitis Foundation Guideline for the Management of Giant Cell Arteritis and Takayasu Arteritis; 2018 EULAR Recommendations for the Management of Large Vessel Vasculitis (published 2020)

### Q9 · Acute ischemic stroke

**Keyed answer: E** — Mechanical thrombectomy

Per the 2019 AHA/ASA acute ischemic stroke update, IV alteplase "should not be administered to patients taking direct thrombin inhibitors or direct factor Xa inhibitors unless laboratory tests such as aPTT, INR, platelet count, ECT, TT, or appropriate direct factor Xa activity assays are normal, or the patient has not received a dose of these agents for >48 hours." A normal INR and aPTT are insensitive to apixaban; only a calibrated anti-Xa activity assay excludes meaningful effect, and this patient took apixaban 6 hours ago — near peak level. Thrombolysis is therefore potentially harmful (Class III: Harm).

Mechanical thrombectomy, however, carries no anticoagulation exclusion. This patient meets every Class I (Level A) criterion: prestroke mRS 0-1, causative occlusion of the ICA or M1, age ≥18, NIHSS ≥6, ASPECTS ≥6, and treatment initiable within 6 hours of last known well. The HERMES pooled analysis demonstrated a large absolute reduction in disability with thrombectomy for anterior circulation LVO. The correct action is immediate transfer to the angiography suite while maintaining BP below 185/110 mm Hg — not laboratory or pharmacologic temporizing that delays reperfusion.

Antithrombotic therapy is deferred until after the procedure and post-procedure imaging.

*Key point:* A DOAC dose within 48 hours contraindicates IV thrombolysis unless a calibrated anti-Xa assay is normal, but it does not contraindicate mechanical thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion. [1]

<details><summary>Why the others are wrong</summary>

- **A** — Andexanet alfa is approved for life-threatening factor Xa inhibitor-associated bleeding, not to create thrombolysis eligibility. No guideline supports reversal-then-lyse in acute ischemic stroke, and it would delay thrombectomy, the proven therapy.
- **B** — Aspirin is appropriate for minor nondisabling stroke without LVO, and generally within 24-48 hours otherwise. Giving it now instead of reperfusion abandons a Class I thrombectomy indication in a patient with NIHSS 17.
- **C** — Alteplase is potentially harmful here: apixaban was taken 6 hours ago, and normal INR/aPTT do not exclude factor Xa inhibitor activity. Only a calibrated anti-Xa assay could clear her, and it is not available.
- **D** — Tenecteplase is a reasonable alteplase alternative in thrombectomy-bound patients, but it is a plasminogen activator and shares every thrombolysis contraindication — including recent direct factor Xa inhibitor exposure.

</details>

*Guidelines cited:* 2019 AHA/ASA Update to the 2018 Guidelines for the Early Management of Patients With Acute Ischemic Stroke; 2021 AHA/ASA Guideline for the Prevention of Stroke in Patients With Stroke and TIA

### Q10 · Perioperative cardiac risk assessment

**Keyed answer: C** — Postpone surgery until 6 months after PCI

The 2024 ACC/AHA perioperative guideline states that elective noncardiac surgery should be delayed at least 6 months after drug-eluting stent implantation, with DAPT continued during that interval; proceeding after 3 months is reasonable only when the surgery is time-sensitive and the risk of further delay outweighs the risk of ischemic events (mirroring the 2016 ACC/AHA DAPT focused update). This patient is 4 months post-DES for stable CAD, is asymptomatic, and needs a purely elective, indefinitely deferrable operation for osteoarthritis. Interrupting clopidogrel now — or operating on full DAPT in a bleeding-risk arthroplasty — accepts avoidable perioperative stent thrombosis or hemorrhage risk when simply waiting 2 more months eliminates the dilemma. Observational data (Hawn, JAMA 2013) show MACE after noncardiac surgery in stented patients concentrates within the first 6 months and tracks with surgical urgency and underlying cardiac risk. Additional ischemic testing is not indicated: he was revascularized 4 months ago, has no angina, and has an unchanged ECG, so imaging would not change management (stress testing is Class 2b only when results would alter care). His limited functional capacity is attributable to knee pain rather than cardiac limitation, which does not by itself mandate testing. The correct management is to time the operation, optimize glycemic control, and continue aspirin, statin, and beta blocker (do not newly start a beta blocker on the day of surgery).

*Key point:* For truly elective noncardiac surgery after drug-eluting stent placement, delay at least 6 months while continuing DAPT; 3 months is acceptable only when surgery is time-sensitive. [1,2]

<details><summary>Why the others are wrong</summary>

- **A** — Stress imaging in an asymptomatic patient revascularized 4 months ago with an unchanged ECG will not change management; the guideline reserves stress testing (Class 2b) for poor/unknown functional capacity when results would alter care. It also mislabels noncardiac (knee) activity limitation as cardiac.
- **B** — Angiography — and any resultant revascularization — is not indicated in stable, asymptomatic, recently stented patients solely to 'clear' them for surgery; prophylactic revascularization before noncardiac surgery does not reduce perioperative MI or death (CARP-era data).
- **D** — Operating on uninterrupted DAPT avoids stent thrombosis but imposes substantial bleeding and transfusion risk in arthroplasty. Because the procedure is entirely elective, the guideline-preferred solution is deferral rather than accepting that bleeding risk now.
- **E** — Stopping clopidogrel at 4 months post-DES for a deferrable operation exposes the patient to perioperative stent thrombosis, an event with roughly 50% mortality. Premature P2Y12 interruption is the classic error this scenario tests.

</details>

*Guidelines cited:* 2024 ACC/AHA/ACS/ASNC/HRS/SCA/SCCT/SCMR/SVM Perioperative Cardiovascular Management for Noncardiac Surgery; 2016 ACC/AHA Focused Update on Duration of Dual Antiplatelet Therapy
